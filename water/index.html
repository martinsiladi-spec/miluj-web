How much of you is water?

Almost all of you. Water molecules are so small and so many that about 99 of every 100 molecules in your body are water. Every other molecule you are made of lives, works and moves in it.

Chemistry tells you what water is made of. Structure tells you how it is arranged, and arrangement can change everything.

In a pencil, carbon atoms form flat hexagonal sheets stacked loosely on top of each other. The sheets slide apart at a touch, which is why graphite is soft enough to write with.

Ordinary water: a restless network

Liquid water is held together by hydrogen bonds that break and re-form trillions of times every second. Its order is fleeting, shaped by temperature, movement, minerals and the surfaces it touches.

Each hydrogen bond lasts about a trillionth of a second
EZ

Structured water: order at the boundary

Next to water-loving surfaces, such as the membranes and proteins inside our cells, water can line up in ordered, hexagonal layers. Researcher Gerald Pollack calls this the exclusion zone, or EZ water, and describes it as a fourth phase of water. Other scientists explain his observations differently; the question is open and actively studied.

The fourth phase · Gerald Pollack

Water that holds charge and moves on its own.

Gerald Pollack, professor of bioengineering at the University of Washington, describes a fourth phase of water between liquid and ice: a gel-like, ordered layer he calls exclusion-zone or EZ water. His lab reports properties that ordinary textbook water does not have.

01

It grows on water-loving surfaces

Ordered water builds up next to hydrophilic materials. In the body they are everywhere: cell membranes, vessel walls, cartilage, collagen and hyaluronan.

02

It holds charge like a battery

The ordered zone carries a negative charge and the water beyond it a positive one. In The Fourth Phase of Water Pollack describes using this separation to light a small LED.

03

It flows without a pump

In a 2013 study his lab placed hydrophilic Nafion tubes in water and saw water stream through them with no pressure difference. Light, especially infrared, made the flow faster.

04

Fields shape water

In 2007 Elmar Fuchs and colleagues at Graz University of Technology showed that a strong electric field makes water form a free-standing bridge in the air between two glasses.

Healthy water, healthy tissue? Korean chemist Mu Shik Jhon went further: in his view healthy tissue is surrounded by ordered water and diseased tissue by disordered water, so drinking ordered water would spare the body the work of ordering it. This is a hypothesis, and one of the ideas behind MILUJ’s approach.

How to read this. These are reported observations and interpretations. Other researchers explain the same experiments with known physics, such as diffusiophoresis, and the existence of a separate phase is not accepted in mainstream science. MILUJ follows the debate openly and builds on it with care.

Inspiration · Viktor Schauberger

Water that moves, lives.

Viktor Schauberger (1885–1958) was an Austrian forester who spent his life watching water in wild mountain streams. Healthy water, he observed, rarely runs straight: it spirals, cools and gathers itself in vortices.

In 1922 he built timber flumes that followed this natural movement and cut the cost of floating logs to a tenth. His patents from 1926 onwards, and his book Implosion statt Explosion (1955), turned the same observation into a principle: inward, spiralling movement builds order, outward explosive force wears it down.

Living water and dead water

Schauberger called water forced through straight pipes and stripped of its movement “dead”, and water allowed to swirl, cool and breathe “living”. In this view water is not just a liquid but a carrier of energy: water that has lost its order asks the body to do the ordering. This is the idea MILUJ builds on, and the one it sets out to measure.

Vortices inside vortices · Theodor Schwenk

In Sensitive Chaos (1962), Theodor Schwenk, who founded an institute for flow research in the Black Forest, showed how water takes the form of its own movement, and how every large vortex carries smaller ones, giving the least resistance and the most exchange. Language remembers it: in German a vertebra is a Wirbel, a vortex, and the spine is the Wirbelsäule, the column of vortices.

Inside the cell · mitochondria

Life runs on hydrogen and water.

Most of your cells hold hundreds to thousands of mitochondria. Zoom in far enough, to single electrons and protons, and the way they make your energy turns out to be a story about hydrogen and water from start to finish.

ElectronProton (H⁺)Water formedATPHydroxyl radicalH₂
  1. 1

    Food is stored hydrogen

    Digestion strips hydrogen from sugars and fats. Enzymes hand it on, as electrons and protons, to carrier molecules (NADH, FADH₂) that deliver it to the mitochondria.

  2. 2

    Electrons tunnel

    In the inner membrane electrons pass along a chain of iron–sulphur and copper centres. They do not flow like current in a wire: they cross the gaps by quantum tunnelling, over distances of up to about 1.4 nanometres.

  3. 3

    Protons ride on water

    The released energy pumps protons, the bare nuclei of hydrogen, across the membrane. Inside the proteins they travel along chains of water molecules, passing from one to the next (the Grotthuss mechanism). Ordered water is part of the machine.

  4. 4

    A spinning turbine

    Protons flow back through ATP synthase, a molecular rotor that turns about 150 times a second and makes three ATP per turn. Every day you produce roughly your own body weight in ATP.

  5. 5

    The last step makes water

    At the end of the chain, oxygen from your breath takes the electrons and protons and becomes water: a few hundred millilitres of fresh metabolic water each day. Breath, hydrogen and water close one circle.

Where molecular hydrogen comes in · emerging research

The chain leaks a little: a few electrons escape and form reactive oxygen species. In 2007 a study in Nature Medicine reported that H₂ selectively neutralises the most aggressive of them, the hydroxyl radical, while leaving useful signalling molecules alone. How far this translates into benefits for people is still being studied.

Why this matters to MILUJ

Water is the medium your mitochondria work in, the highway their protons travel on and the product they make. That is why MILUJ treats water, its structure and the hydrogen it carries as one subject, and why we want to measure what our water does rather than assume it.

What water can carry

Hydrogen, held long enough to matter.

Molecular hydrogen (H₂) is the smallest molecule there is. Research is exploring its role in oxidative signalling, metabolism and recovery; the field is promising, and still developing. Its practical problem is simple: H₂ escapes from water quickly once a bottle is opened.

Fascia · the body’s riverbeds

Rivers inside you.

Fascia is one continuous web of connective tissue, from head to toe, wrapping every muscle, organ and nerve. Fluid runs between its layers: in 2018 researchers described fluid-filled spaces throughout this tissue, a network of channels draining towards the lymph. These are the body’s riverbeds.

Water is what lets fascia glide. Hyaluronan between its layers binds water into a slippery gel; when the tissue dries out, layers stick and movement stiffens. Stretching squeezes water out of fascia like a sponge, and in laboratory studies the tissue then soaks up more than before (Schleip et al., 2012).

That is why it matters what we fill these rivers with. The water you drink becomes the fluid around your cells within hours. MILUJ’s view is that it should be the best water we can give: clean, mineral-balanced, structured and rich in hydrogen. We will follow tissue hydration with measurements such as bioimpedance before and after a stay, rather than take it on faith.

Seen alive

French hand surgeon Jean-Claude Guimberteau filmed living connective tissue under the skin with a micro-camera: a shimmering web of fine fibres, held in a gel that is mostly water, constantly reshaping as we move.

Held by tension

Fascia is often described with Buckminster Fuller’s principle of tensegrity: a structure kept in balance by continuous tension and local compression. A well-hydrated web absorbs shocks and spreads force through the whole body.

A sensing web

Fascia is densely supplied with sensory nerve endings; researcher Robert Schleip calls it one of our richest sensory organs. When it dries out and stops gliding, layers can “glue” together, one proposed source of stiffness and of pain felt far from where it starts.

Drinking alone is not enough. Fascia takes up water when it moves: stretching, dance, breath, massage and warmth squeeze and refill it like a sponge. That is why at MILUJ the water and the movement practices belong together.

Established physiology

Water is the medium for every process inside and between our cells, and normal hydration is essential to human function. Molecular structure changes the properties of matter, as carbon shows.

Emerging research

Molecular hydrogen is being studied for effects on oxidative signalling and cellular processes; clinical evidence is still developing. Ordered water near surfaces (EZ water) is reported and debated.
